\begin{abstract}
Heavy rainfall and extreme storm events cause frequent floods and landslides in mountain valleys. This study developed an operational machine learning framework to analyze daily rainfall patterns and classify precipitation intensity across the Kathmandu watershed. Ten complete years of daily ground measurements (2016--2025; $69{,}407$ station-days) from 19 Department of Hydrology and Meteorology (DHM) stations were integrated with NASA Global Precipitation Measurement (GPM) IMERG V07 satellite estimates. Direct statistical validation revealed that raw satellite estimates systematically overestimated light rainfall with a mean bias of $+2.78\text{ mm/day}$ and exhibited a high false alarm ratio ($\text{FAR} = 46.12\%$). Unsupervised Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and K-Means clustering identified three distinct microclimatic rainfall zones governed by station elevation ($K=3$, Silhouette score $0.584$). To tackle severe class imbalance ($86.11\%$ light rain vs. $0.11\%$ combined very heavy and extreme events), regularized Random Forest, XGBoost, and feature-scaled Support Vector Machines (Linear and RBF kernels) were trained using a chronological split (2016--2022 training, 2023--2024 validation, and 2025 independent test) alongside Leave-One-District-Out spatial testing. On the independent 2025 test set ($N = 6{,}935$), feature-scaled Linear SVM delivered the highest overall accuracy ($74.72\%$, weighted F1: $78.92\%$, macro F1: $0.3682$). Random Forest attained the highest balanced accuracy ($59.80\%$, macro F1: $0.3446$), correctly identifying $80.00\%$ of very heavy storms. XGBoost achieved $70.48\%$ overall accuracy, the highest heavy rain recall ($42.65\%$), and the highest Macro ROC-AUC ($0.88$). Spatial cross-validation confirmed stable generalization across Kathmandu, Lalitpur, and Bhaktapur districts ($71.21\%$--$75.85\%$ accuracy). SHAP feature attribution demonstrated that antecedent rainfall memory from the previous 1 to 3 days exerted the greatest physical influence on model predictions ($74.47\%$ relative gain for 1-day lag; $9.47\%$ for 3-day lag). Non-parametric Mann--Kendall trend tests confirmed a statistically significant rise in annual average rainfall ($\tau = +0.5111$, $p = 0.0491$, $Q = +0.2012\text{ mm/year}$), while daily maximums ($+2.4800\text{ mm/year}$) and extreme events ($+0.8889\text{ events/year}$) exhibited marked upward trends, offering valuable guidance for municipal flood warnings and disaster mitigation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Problem Statement}
\label{sec:introduction}
Precipitation extremes in the Central Himalayan region represent severe hydrometeorological hazards, triggering recurrent flash floods, destructive debris flows, and extensive urban inundation. The Kathmandu watershed (Upper Bagmati Basin), which accommodates over 2.5 million inhabitants within an area of approximately $650\text{ km}^2$, is exceptionally susceptible to catastrophic hydrometeorological events. This acute vulnerability stems from rapid, unplanned urban expansion, dense settlements along natural floodplains, and complex bowl-shaped topography spanning elevations from 1,280 meters above sea level (masl) on the valley floor to over 2,150 masl along surrounding mountain ridges. During the South Asian Summer Monsoon (June to September), moist air masses from the Bay of Bengal encounter these steep topographic barriers, generating intense orographic convection and localized cloudbursts that rapidly overwhelm natural drainage channels.

Effective disaster mitigation and municipal risk reduction require timely, highly reliable daily rainfall estimates. Ground-based weather radars remain unavailable in the Kathmandu Basin, and terrestrial tipping-bucket rain gauges operated by the Department of Hydrology and Meteorology (DHM) are irregularly distributed across mountain slopes. While satellite products like NASA's Global Precipitation Measurement (GPM) IMERG provide continuous spatial coverage, spaceborne passive microwave and infrared sensors experience significant retrieval errors over rugged topography due to ground clutter, high surface emissivity variations, and cloud-top height uncertainties.

As synthesized in the Problem Statement Diagram (Fig.~\ref{fig:problem_statement}), three primary challenges impede dependable precipitation modeling and hazard forecasting in the Kathmandu watershed:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Substantial Satellite Estimation Bias in Mountain Basins:} Spaceborne precipitation estimates systematically overestimate light drizzle while underestimating localized convective cloudbursts along steep ridgelines, leading to frequent false alarms and missed flood-inducing storms.
    \item \textbf{Shifting Decadal Hydrometeorological Regimes:} Historical rainfall baselines are shifting under regional climate variability, necessitating rigorous non-parametric trend detection to quantify decadal intensification in storm volume and frequency.
    \item \textbf{Severe Observational Class Imbalance:} Life-threatening extreme cloudbursts comprise less than $0.15\%$ of total daily observations, causing standard machine learning algorithms to exhibit strong majority-class bias toward non-hazardous light rain.
\end{enumerate}

To overcome these operational challenges, this research established an integrated computational framework combining 10 years of synchronous ground gauge observations with satellite estimates, utilizing unsupervised clustering to delineate microclimatic zones and supervised machine learning algorithms to predict multi-class rainfall intensity.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature}
The integration of satellite-derived precipitation estimates with terrestrial gauge observations constitutes an active research domain in mountain hydrometeorology. Satellite products provide continuous gridded monitoring over inaccessible terrains, but rugged topography introduces substantial retrieval errors that necessitate machine learning calibration.

\subsection{Analysis of Core Base Papers and Regional Benchmarks}
This investigation was built upon three foundational base papers and five supporting regional and methodological benchmarks (summarized in Table~\ref{tab:lit_comparison}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Base Paper 1 (Sharma et al. \cite{sharma2020}):} Evaluated GPM IMERG satellite precipitation across diverse topographic regions of Nepal against terrestrial observations, establishing that satellite products exhibit high False Alarm Ratios ($\text{FAR} > 40\%$) and elevation-dependent biases across Himalayan basins.
    \item \textbf{Base Paper 2 (Khanal et al. \cite{khanal2024}):} Analyzed decadal rainfall trends across Nepal's watersheds over three decades, identifying statistically significant increases in extreme storm frequency and daily precipitation maximums using Mann--Kendall tests.
    \item \textbf{Base Paper 3 (Lei et al. \cite{lei2025}):} Benchmarked nine machine learning algorithms for downscaling and calibrating IMERG precipitation, demonstrating that non-linear ensemble techniques outperform linear statistical methods but noting a lack of physical interpretability.
    \item \textbf{Supporting Literature:} Tang et al. \cite{Tang2020} and Huffman et al. \cite{Huffman2020} validated the global performance and calibration algorithms of GPM IMERG V07. Breiman \cite{Breiman2001} and Chen and Guestrin \cite{Chen2016} established the algorithmic foundations for Random Forest and XGBoost. Lundberg and Lee \cite{Lundberg2017} developed the unified SHAP framework for Explainable Artificial Intelligence. Regional studies by Lamichhane et al. \cite{lamichhane2025} and Talchabhadel et al. \cite{talchabhadel2022} documented the devastating hydrometeorological impacts of recent extreme cloudbursts in the Kathmandu Valley, including the catastrophic September 2024 flood disaster.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Identification of Addressed Research Gaps}
Critical examination of existing literature identified three core research gaps that directly motivated the methodology of this study:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Gap 1 (Originating from Sharma et al. \cite{sharma2020}):} Prior satellite evaluations focused on large-scale regional averages that smoothed out localized valley microclimates. There is an absence of point-to-grid machine learning frameworks incorporating station elevation and geographic coordinates to correct satellite bias at individual station locations across complex mountain basins \cite{karki2017}.
    \item \textbf{Gap 2 (Originating from Khanal et al. \cite{khanal2024}):} Climatological studies have examined historical rainfall trends as isolated statistical time-series without connecting detected decadal shifts to forward-looking, multi-class predictive intensity forecasting \cite{zhu2023, callau2023}.
    \item \textbf{Gap 3 (Originating from Lei et al. \cite{lei2025}):} Previous machine learning applications treated models as opaque black boxes without explaining the underlying physical mechanisms. There is a need to unite unsupervised spatial clustering with SHAP feature attribution to explain how antecedent precipitation memory and topography govern rainfall predictions \cite{he2009, talchabhadel2021}.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Theoretical Framework}
\label{sec:theory}
This study implemented five machine learning and statistical algorithms to analyze spatial regimes and classify precipitation intensity, alongside non-parametric trend tests and satellite validation metrics.

\subsection{Unsupervised Spatial Clustering: PCA and K-Means}
\textbf{Principal Component Analysis (PCA):} PCA reduces feature dimensionality while preserving maximum spatial variance. Input station vectors $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^p$ are standardized to zero mean and unit variance ($\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) / \boldsymbol{\sigma}$). The sample covariance matrix $\mathbf{\Sigma}$ and eigenvalue decomposition are computed as:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{\Sigma} = \frac{1}{n-1} \tilde{\mathbf{X}}^\top \tilde{\mathbf{X}}, \quad \mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{v}_i = \lambda_i \mathbf{v}_i
\label{eq:pca_cov}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_i$ represents the $i$-th eigenvalue indicating explained variance, and $\mathbf{v}_i$ is the corresponding orthogonal eigenvector. Projecting standardized features onto the top $k$ eigenvectors yields low-dimensional coordinates $\mathbf{Z} = \tilde{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{V}_k$.

\textbf{K-Means Clustering:} K-Means partitions $n$ station feature vectors $\mathbf{z}_i$ into $K$ disjoint clusters $S = \{S_1, S_2, \dots, S_K\}$ by iteratively minimizing the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS):
\begin{equation}
J = \sum_{j=1}^{K} \sum_{i=1}^{n} || \mathbf{x}_i^{(j)} - \mathbf{c}_j ||^2
\label{eq:kmeans}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}_i^{(j)}$ represents sample $i$ belonging to cluster $j$, and $\mathbf{c}_j$ denotes the centroid of cluster $j$. Optimal cluster separation was validated using the Silhouette Coefficient $s(i) = \frac{b(i) - a(i)}{\max(a(i), b(i))}$, where $a(i)$ is the mean intra-cluster distance and $b(i)$ is the mean nearest-cluster distance.

\subsection{Supervised Classification Algorithms}
\textbf{Random Forest:} An ensemble of $B$ decision trees trained on bootstrap samples with random feature subsampling. Node splits maximize Gini impurity reduction:
\begin{equation}
I_G(t) = 1 - \sum_{c=1}^{C} p(c|t)^2
\label{eq:gini}
\end{equation}
where $p(c|t)$ is the proportion of class $c$ samples at node $t$. Final class predictions are obtained through majority voting across all ensemble trees: $\hat{y} = \arg\max_c \sum_{b=1}^{B} I(\hat{y}_b = c)$.

\textbf{XGBoost:} XGBoost minimizes an objective function combining regularized loss and tree complexity:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}^{(t)} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} l\left(y_i, \hat{y}_i^{(t-1)} + f_t(\mathbf{x}_i)\right) + \Omega(f_t)
\label{eq:xgboost_obj}
\end{equation}
where $l$ denotes the multi-class log-loss function measuring prediction error, $\hat{y}_i^{(t-1)}$ is the prediction at step $t-1$, and $f_t(\mathbf{x}_i)$ is the newly added decision tree. The model complexity penalty $\Omega(f)$ is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
\Omega(f) = \gamma T + \frac{1}{2} \lambda ||\mathbf{w}||^2 + \alpha ||\mathbf{w}||_1
\label{eq:xgboost_reg}
\end{equation}
where $T$ represents the total number of terminal leaves, $\mathbf{w}$ is the vector of leaf weights, $\gamma$ is the leaf penalty parameter, and $\lambda, \alpha$ represent $L_2$ and $L_1$ regularization terms preventing overfitting.

\textbf{Support Vector Machines (Linear and RBF Kernels):} SVM seeks an optimal separating hyperplane that maximizes the functional margin between classes. In the dual formulation with Lagrange multipliers $\alpha_i$, the optimization problem is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} \sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_i - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j y_i y_j K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)
\label{eq:svm_dual}
\end{equation}
subject to $0 \le \alpha_i \le C$ and $\sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i y_i = 0$, where $C$ is the regularization penalty balancing margin width against training errors. Both the Linear Kernel $K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) = \mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j$ and Radial Basis Function (RBF) Kernel $K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) = \exp(-\gamma \|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|^2)$ were implemented, where $\gamma$ determines the influence radius of individual support vectors.

\subsection{Antecedent Rainfall and Validation Formulations}
To incorporate physical catchment wetness and hydrologic memory, antecedent precipitation rolling sums over 3-day and 7-day windows were calculated:
\begin{equation}
R_{3,t} = \sum_{i=1}^{3} R_{t-i}, \qquad R_{7,t} = \sum_{i=1}^{7} R_{t-i}
\label{eq:antecedent_lags}
\end{equation}
where $R_{t-i}$ denotes recorded precipitation on day $t-i$.

Satellite estimation accuracy relative to ground gauges was evaluated using the Pearson Correlation Coefficient ($\text{CC}$):
\begin{equation}
\text{CC} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (G_i - \bar{G})(S_i - \bar{S})}{\left[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} (G_i - \bar{G})^2 \sum_{i=1}^{n} (S_i - \bar{S})^2 \right]^{1/2}}
\label{eq:cc}
\end{equation}
where $G_i$ represents ground gauge observations, $S_i$ represents satellite estimates, and $\bar{G}, \bar{S}$ are their sample means. Continuous error magnitudes were quantified using Root Mean Square Error ($\text{RMSE}$) and Mean Bias ($\text{MB}$):
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\text{RMSE} &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (S_i - G_i)^2}, \\
\text{MB} &= \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (S_i - G_i)
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:rmse_mb}
\end{equation}
Categorical contingency performance was assessed via Probability of Detection ($\text{POD}$), False Alarm Ratio ($\text{FAR}$), and Critical Success Index ($\text{CSI}$):
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\text{POD} &= \frac{H}{H + M}, \quad \text{FAR} = \frac{F}{H + F}, \\
\text{CSI} &= \frac{H}{H + M + F}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:pod_far_csi}
\end{equation}
where $H$ represents successful hits, $M$ represents missed storm events, and $F$ represents false alarms.

Decadal monotonic trends were quantified using the non-parametric Mann--Kendall test statistic $S$ \cite{gorelick2017, pedregosa2011}:
\begin{equation}
S = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \sum_{j=i+1}^{n} \operatorname{sgn}(x_j - x_i)
\label{eq:mk_s}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{sgn}(\theta)$ equals $+1, 0,$ or $-1$ depending on whether the difference is positive, zero, or negative. Kendall's rank correlation coefficient is $\tau = S / [n(n-1)/2]$. Trend magnitude was determined via Sen's slope estimator $Q$ \cite{sen1968}:
\begin{equation}
Q = \operatorname{median}\left( \frac{x_j - x_i}{j - i} \right), \quad \forall j > i
\label{eq:sen_slope}
\end{equation}
Statistical significance was established at $\alpha = 0.05$. Finally, model predictions were interpreted using SHAP additive feature attribution \cite{scirep2024, taylor2001}:
\begin{equation}
f(x) = \phi_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{M} \phi_j(x)
\label{eq:shap}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_0$ is the base expected model output and $\phi_j(x)$ represents the marginal Shapley attribution for feature $j$.

\subsection{Feature Engineering and Model Configurations}
Eight predictive features were extracted: daily satellite rainfall ($P_t$), multi-day antecedent rainfall rolling sums ($P_{t-1}, P_{t-3}, P_{t-7}$), station elevation, latitude, longitude, and calendar month.

To evaluate generalization and prevent temporal data leakage, records were split chronologically:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training Set (2016--2022):} 7 years, $N = 48{,}583$ samples ($70.0\%$).
    \item \textbf{Validation Set (2023--2024):} 2 years, $N = 13{,}889$ samples ($20.0\%$).
    \item \textbf{Independent Test Set (2025):} 1 year, $N = 6{,}935$ samples ($10.0\%$).
\end{itemize}
In addition, Leave-One-District-Out (LODO) spatial cross-validation was conducted across Kathmandu, Lalitpur, and Bhaktapur districts to evaluate spatial generalization to unmonitored sectors. Hyperparameters were tuned to prevent overfitting while handling class imbalance (Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The experimental results demonstrate clear operational trade-offs for meteorological early warning systems. Feature-scaled Linear SVM yielded the highest overall accuracy ($74.72\%$) and lowest computational latency, making it optimal for routine daily weather advisories where false alarms must be minimized. In contrast, XGBoost and Random Forest excelled at capturing rare, high-consequence precipitation: XGBoost reached a Macro ROC-AUC of $0.88$ and $42.65\%$ recall on Class 2 heavy storms, while Random Forest captured $80.00\%$ of Class 3 very heavy cloudbursts.

SHAP feature attribution confirmed that antecedent rainfall memory ($P_{t-1}, P_{t-3}$) is the dominant physical driver of daily precipitation classification. Mountain storms in the Kathmandu watershed exhibit multi-day persistence, where prior-day rainfall primes atmospheric humidity and catchment saturation.

However, extreme class imbalance ($0.11\%$ combined representation for Classes 3 and 4) remains an operational bottleneck. While balanced class weighting successfully shifted classification hyperplanes, future operational deployments must integrate synthetic oversampling (SMOTE) with physics-based Numerical Weather Prediction models to forecast unprecedented cloudbursts.


\section{Social, Ethical, Legal, and Professional Considerations}
\label{sec:social_ethical}
Over 2.5 million residents in the Kathmandu Valley face recurrent monsoon flooding, with informal riverside settlements suffering disproportionate exposure. Developing an accurate, localized machine learning classification framework directly supports municipal disaster risk reduction.

In natural hazard forecasting, prediction errors carry asymmetric consequences: a false alarm causes minor inconvenience, whereas a false negative (failing to predict an extreme storm) results in catastrophic loss of life. Consequently, prioritizing model recall on heavy rain over raw light-rain accuracy is an ethical imperative.

All data utilized in this study was accessed under open research licenses from DHM Nepal and NASA's Earth Science Data System. Computational workflows and model parameters are fully documented to uphold scientific reproducibility.


\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This research developed a machine learning framework for daily rainfall classification and decadal trend detection across the Kathmandu watershed, integrating 10 years of synchronous ground gauge data ($69{,}407$ station-days) with GPM IMERG satellite estimates. The findings directly resolve the three core research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Satellite Reliability:} Raw GPM IMERG V07 satellite rainfall systematically overestimates daily rainfall ($\text{MB} = +2.78\text{ mm/day}$) and exhibits a $46.12\%$ False Alarm Ratio over mountain terrain, confirming the necessity of ground calibration.
    \item \textbf{Decadal Trend Shifts:} Mann--Kendall tests confirmed statistically significant increases in annual rainfall ($+0.2012\text{ mm/yr}, p = 0.0491$) and heavy rain days ($+0.0820\text{ days/yr}, p = 0.0210$), proving that extreme precipitation events are intensifying.
    \item \textbf{Classification Performance and Physical Drivers:} On the 2025 independent test set ($N = 6{,}935$), feature-scaled Linear SVM achieved $74.72\%$ accuracy, while XGBoost delivered superior heavy storm discrimination ($0.88$ Macro ROC-AUC, $42.65\%$ Class 2 recall). SHAP analysis confirmed that antecedent rainfall memory ($P_{t-1}$) is the primary physical predictor ($74.47\%$ relative gain).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Limitations and Future Work:} The study is constrained by 10 years of daily records and lacks terrestrial stations above 2,200 masl. Future work should integrate high-resolution X-band dual-polarization weather radar, evaluate recurrent deep architectures (LSTM/TCN) for hourly sub-daily forecasting, and deploy solar-powered IoT tipping-bucket rain gauges across unmonitored ridgelines to power automated municipal flood warning systems.
